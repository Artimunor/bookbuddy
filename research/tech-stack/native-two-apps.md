# Two native apps: SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose

**Decision as of 23 November 2026: ship a shared mobile UI and business logic, with native code for lifecycle-sensitive features. Do not start with two independent mobile implementations.** SwiftUI and Jetpack Compose can deliver every requested feature. Their strongest advantage here is direct control of capture, uploads and OS lifecycle events, not the booking screens themselves. Start by proving those risky paths on both platforms; replace a failing adapter or screen natively before considering a second full implementation. Keep the operations web portal a distinct product surface in either case.

This recommendation assumes a small team, a two-platform pilot, rapidly changing workflows and no existing mobile codebase or committed iOS/Android specialists. It compares two independent native clients with a shared-UI native-binary app (such as React Native/Expo or Flutter) and Kotlin Multiplatform (KMP) with separate native UIs. It does not treat a browser app as equivalent to either mobile option. [Product brief](../../docs/reports/2026-11-21-builders-company-idea.md) · [Idea material](../idea/idea-points.md). Dates on articles and datasets refer to publication, fielding or observation as indicated. Undated living API and policy documentation below was consulted on 23 November 2026.

### Store review and physical-service payments

[Apple App Review Guidelines §3.1.3(e)](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#goods-and-services) requires a method other than In-App Purchase for physical goods or services consumed outside the app. [Google Play Payments §3](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9858738?hl=en) says Play Billing must not be used where payment is primarily for physical services. This covers a real repair or in-person booking in the stated model, not a paid unlock for foreman software, premium translation or digital content. Classify any later digital subscription, remote sessions or mixed bundle separately against each platform's then-current storefront rules. Apple Pay and Google Pay are wallets, not the two stores' digital billing systems.

A PSP integration doesn't settle who contracts with the customer or collects, refunds and pays out funds. Decide that operating model before implementing split payments or holding funds. Provide reviewers with a working account and an explanation of what is delivered in person. Treat customer/crew chat and media as user-generated content in review planning: design reporting, blocking and an operator moderation process against [Apple §1.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#user-generated-content) and [Google Play's UGC policy](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/9876937?hl=en). Minimize media and location permissions on both stacks. For Spain distribution, [Apple's requirements](https://developer.apple.com/news/upcoming-requirements/) include verified EU trader-status disclosure where applicable (enforced from February 2025). Confirm policy and PSP terms again before submission. Actual review and the Spain payments/contracting model are **[unverified]**.

## Platform fit, accessibility and languages

SwiftUI and Compose make it easiest to start from [Apple's Human Interface Guidelines](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/) and [Android's Material 3 implementation](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/designsystems/material3). Preserve the same job actions and wording, not identical pixels. Let iOS and Android use their expected navigation, back behavior, sheets, system pickers and text entry. Shared UI can do this by adapting those surfaces deliberately rather than duplicating every form. Native is the better default if matching each OS's newest interaction patterns is a release requirement across much of the app.

Native controls give a strong starting point for [VoiceOver and Dynamic Type in SwiftUI](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10073/) (WWDC24) and [TalkBack semantics in Compose](https://developer.android.com/develop/ui/compose/accessibility/semantics). They do not guarantee accessible custom chat bubbles, job timelines or map pins. Keep touch targets and text large, don't encode status in color alone, and verify focus order, font scaling, screen-reader labels, pending/delivered announcements and the record/play controls on both OSes. Test an older or low-memory Android device actually used by a crew, not only a flagship. Customer and crew device mix is **[unverified]**.

Separate interface locale from preferred message language. Use one canonical set of translation keys and terminology; compile to [Xcode String Catalogs](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/xcode/localizing-and-varying-text-with-a-string-catalog), [Android localized resources](https://developer.android.com/training/basics/supporting-devices/languages) and web messages as needed. Test [right-to-left layout](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/right-to-left) even if the first cohort has no RTL UI locale: an English or Spanish interface can still contain Arabic or Hebrew messages. Align original and translation independently by their content, isolate mixed-script values such as addresses, numbers and identifiers, mirror directional controls selectively, and verify speech language and reading order. Localize formatting, plurals and dates, not addresses or job identifiers. Both approaches need these checks; two native UIs need two implementations of this presentation behavior.

## Small-team cost, time and hiring

- **Count implementations, not repositories.** Dual native has two mobile feature implementations, two platform integrations, a separate responsive portal and one backend. One monorepo is possible; it doesn't make Swift and Kotlin screen logic shared. Shared mobile UI removes the second implementation for booking, changes, rosters, scheduling, chat presentation and onboarding. It still needs two binaries, signing setups, store reviews and real-device test lanes, plus work on upgrades and adapters. KMP with native UI saves some logic, not the second implementation of each screen.
- **Estimate the delta on the same pilot scope.** Backend, translation, compliance, local operations and a dispatcher/owner portal are broadly common costs. For each pilot feature count product UI/client behavior, device integration, mobile QA/release and future version upgrades separately. Native adds the second UI/client implementation and parity maintenance; shared UI instead adds framework upkeep, adapter gaps and native debugging at the boundary. Native is cheaper only if the avoided framework/adapter effort and available team expertise outweigh that duplicate implementation and drift. Don't apply a blanket 2× multiplier to team or delivery cost. A project-specific person-week or euro estimate is **[unverified]** without scope and staffing quotes.
- **Time to market depends on parallel staffing.** With one mobile engineer, the work on the second native UI is mostly sequential; two specialists can do it in parallel, but the two-platform pilot still waits for the slower release and shared backend. A shared team can change one enable/decline flow after field feedback and run it against both clients, rather than first arranging a parity handoff. A responsive web portal should cover dispatcher work and useful link fallbacks, not match every field app feature. For a two-native choice, keep one API and state-transition contract, shared low-cost fixtures and acceptance scenarios, and a parity checklist per release. Two binaries must still track platform sunsets; examples are [Apple's April 2026 minimum SDK and Xcode requirements](https://developer.apple.com/news/upcoming-requirements/) and [Play's August 2026 API 36 target rule](https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/11926878?hl=en).
- **Hire for ownership.** Two native apps need dependable Swift/iOS and Kotlin/Android coverage plus web/backend capability. Those are skills, not necessarily three hires, but relying on one person for all platform emergencies is fragile. In a shared-UI team, assign one clear mobile release owner, with access to Swift and Kotlin expertise for OS/SDK changes and incidents; make sure there is cover for both release tracks. React/TypeScript may let a web-skilled engineer contribute to mobile and share types, validation and localization tooling with a web portal. Flutter/Dart offers mobile UI reuse, but with a separate TypeScript portal the practical overlap is mostly contracts and assets. Use demonstrated app-store releases, background-work debugging and offline data handling as hiring signals, not keyword counts. The surveys below don't establish availability or salary by framework in Alicante or for this team **[unverified]**.

## When to choose native and how to keep options open

**Choose a native subsystem** when a pilot shows a concrete gap: a wrapper drops uploads after lifecycle transitions, cannot surface progress or completion reliably, delays necessary OS API support, or sends large media through the shared runtime. Let Swift/Kotlin own OS transfer jobs and file handles; expose enqueue, progress, cancellation and completion to the shared app without copying media through the bridge. Keep booking, translation and role behavior out of those adapters. [Expo custom native code and development builds](https://docs.expo.dev/workflow/customizing) and [Flutter platform channels and typed Pigeon bindings](https://docs.flutter.dev/platform-integration/platform-channels) make this a viable middle path. Budget for native rebuilds and debugging; don't assume an over-the-air update can replace a store-reviewed binary change or bypass [Apple §2.5.2](https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/#software-requirements).

**Choose both native apps** if platform-specific behavior is the product, not one integration: for example, the core field workflow needs custom high-throughput capture, on-device computer vision or hardware integration, combined with independently designed OS experiences, extensions or strict low-end performance budgets. Direct access is also a material advantage if OS upgrades repeatedly break multiple critical paths in the shared stack, or two already-staffed native teams can ship and maintain parity more economically. No app framework can override permissions, push delivery or background scheduling limits. The current BookBuddy brief does not establish such a reason; a measured failure across critical flows would be stronger evidence than a synthetic benchmark.

**Choose KMP with native UI** when SwiftUI and Compose presentation is worth keeping, but client rules are substantial enough to share. Put sync/outbox state transitions, API models and client validation in common Kotlin; keep views, transfer scheduling, camera, credentials and permissions in platform code. This keeps UI/platform control and limits sync drift, but both UIs and store releases remain. Budget for Gradle–Xcode integration, a small Swift-facing API, and cancellation/error mapping. [JetBrains' native-UI shared-logic tutorial](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/multiplatform/multiplatform-create-first-app.html) (updated 1 October 2026) documents the pattern; JetBrains labels [direct Swift export](https://kotlinlang.org/docs/native-swift-export.html) **Alpha**, not a premise to depend on without validation. KMP is less attractive if the backend already owns most rules and the repeated cost is screens, not sync logic.

**Keep a migration boundary, not a migration programme.** A shared-UI client can replace a single screen or subsystem natively. An existing native app can embed Flutter for a bounded flow, as Malt did, but splitting navigation, sessions and build ownership between runtimes adds costs; don't start greenfield with two app shells just in case. Keep stable HTTPS routes and a well-specified backend API so any later transition preserves app links, pushed offers, session continuity and pending work.

### What would change this recommendation in the pilot

On a representative older Android device and a supported iPhone, exercise a job open from a push, a check-in with no signal, and a photo plus voice note queued in airplane mode. Then exercise reconnect, suspension, OS process death distinct from user force-quit, and reopen. Neither client should lose a locally accepted draft, post a duplicate, call a pending shift confirmed, or claim an attachment was sent before server acknowledgment. Record cold start to the assigned-job list, transfer completion or recoverability by lifecycle state, and VoiceOver/TalkBack access to the translated-chat and pending states. Set performance thresholds from crew/device evidence; none have been measured **[unverified]**. If one subsystem fails and the direct native path passes, switch that subsystem and measure again. Reconsider the two-app decision only if critical failures span several workflows or staffing changes materially.

## Scored summary for the first two-store pilot

Scores are decision judgments, not measurements: **1** = poor fit, **3** = workable with significant trade-offs, **5** = best fit under the stated assumptions. Weighted points = weight × score ÷ 5. The shared-UI column assumes a native binary on each OS, a separate portal, a named release owner and a verified adapter or small native module for background transfers. A critical field workflow that cannot pass the pilot is a veto regardless of the total.

| Criterion | Weight | Two native apps | Shared mobile UI + native adapters | KMP logic + native UIs |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Time to two-platform launch and field iteration | 24 | 2 | 5 | 3 |
| Maintenance cost, including separate web portal and parity | 18 | 2 | 4 | 3 |
| Device integrations, background execution and performance control | 16 | 5 | 4 | 5 |
| Platform conventions, accessibility and RTL fit | 14 | 5 | 4 | 5 |
| Offline sync, chat and workflow consistency | 12 | 4 | 5 | 5 |
| Hiring flexibility and release ownership | 10 | 3 | 4 | 3 |
| Store rules, payments and distribution | 6 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| **Weighted total / 100** | **100** | **67.2** | **87.2** | **78.0** |

The native score is not a capability penalty: it buys the most direct device control and OS-specific UI at the cost of building both clients. If 20 weight points move from launch speed to device/OS control, KMP with native UIs leads **86.0 to 83.2** over shared UI; independent native rises to **79.2**. On current evidence, the pilot should buy native control only where it improves a field workflow, while keeping the handoff, schedule and conversation behavior shared.
